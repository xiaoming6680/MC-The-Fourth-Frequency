# The Fourth Frequency — World bible

The current narrative boundaries of `1.0.0-rc.1`. Numbers and tests are owned by [The World Interface finale](world-interface.md) and [Testing and acceptance](testing.md).

## Premise

The world was not suddenly invaded by an outside monster. It has always been maintaining a comprehensible surface out of the blocks, menus, sounds and rules the player already knows, and "the fourth frequency" is the communication noise that surface cannot fully suppress.

The terminal is not a quest-log wrapper. It is the interface through which the world explains itself on limited bandwidth. It first learns to record the player, then learns to answer; and the more accurate its answers become, the harder it is for the world to keep pretending everything is normal.

## Facts that cannot be rewritten

- Station Zero and the terminal are the entrance to the story, and the terminal is the player's only stable instrument of explanation.
- The survival mainline is still built on vanilla actions: wood, iron, the Nether, blaze rods, eyes of ender, the stronghold and the End.
- The four damaged files come from investigating vanilla world features. They do not depend on extra generated "installations".
- Anomalies are not a bag of random easter eggs. They move through tiers 1–5, from environmental error toward the interface and rule layers.
- The Corrector learns the player's sound, routes, predictions, space and interface across five personal forms. It is the evidence that the World Interface is learning *how to use a body* — not a low-health version of the final enemy.
- A real pursuit happens in a private correction layer, isolated from other players. The loneliness comes from the observer being temporarily lifted out of shared reality, not from other players pretending not to see them.
- The final battle is a ritual in which everyone submits their right of explanation: every participant must hand over their own bound terminal.

## The experience curve

### 1. A believable old version

On first world entry the client completes an Alpha-style downgrade: resource packs are managed as Programmer Art → Golden Days Base → Golden Days Alpha, and the loading screen and version strings pull the familiar game back into a not-entirely-real "Minecraft 1.0.0".

It should first make players doubt the version and their own memory, not announce a horror theme.

### 2. Repeatable error

Anomalies begin as small deviations in sound, light, blocks and records. The server decides only *what happened*; the client decides *how the target player perceives it*. Multiplayer can share the discovery without sharing the same hallucination.

The terminal's voice stays short, calm, like an instrument working hard to keep its noise floor down. It may be incomplete, but it must never draw the player's conclusions for them in an omniscient narrator's voice.

### 3. The world starts recording the player

The shelter, mineral, portal, weather, navigation and stronghold tools gradually turn from scanners into testimony. The three real eye-of-ender throws matter most: the World Interface is not handing over coordinates out of nowhere; it is acknowledging that the player has already proved a direction.

The weather tool is the earliest and most easily missed notch on this curve. It exists from the start and normally just reports weather and time. But it monitors four channels of the sky at the same time, so when the sky starts being rewritten it is the first thing that knows — and the first thing that starts losing signal.

The hidden file in `FILES` links scattered phenomena into one body/interface mapping while leaving room for interpretation. The player should gradually realise that "anomaly" may just be the moment the world can no longer describe itself in vanilla vocabulary.

### 4. The private correction layer

An ordinary anomaly first demonstrates the next Corrector form's rules safely; the terminal then translates that observation into a short, actionable warning. Only the real pursuit moves the player into the private mirror of their current dimension.

The mirror is not another world to mine. It copies the reality near the player and keeps unfolding along their escape route, but containers, loot, other players and real progress are stripped out. The player can clear a path and place things temporarily, and can bring nothing back out of false terrain. The narrative boundary here is:

> The world is not real. The damage is.

The five forms are not simply "faster":

- Sound-Seeker reads repeated action as position;
- Router reads habitual routes as intent;
- Interceptor reads movement trend as a future exit;
- Trespasser stops accepting walls and ceilings as boundaries;
- The Interface Corrector begins forging coordinates, text and direction; the heartbeat only says where it is and how close, never where the exit is.

Racing ahead through the mainline is not punished with back-to-back pursuits. The world keeps only the *next* correction opportunity, and every form must first be seen and explained by the terminal before it becomes a real confrontation.

### 5. Handing over the terminals together

The End altar sits flush with the native main island, because the finale has to happen in the world the player knows. Twenty inert gateways read as exits nobody turned on; ten stability anchors read as the world's local promise that it is still refusing to collapse.

Freezing the roster and submitting terminal by terminal carries meaning: no single client can unilaterally represent all observers. Withdrawal is possible before submission; after it, everyone is inside the same transaction.

## "The Fourth Frequency · World Interface"

The World Interface is not a body in the conventional sense. The three forms are the same interface progressively giving up legibility under pressure; forms only ever advance and never regress on healing.

Stability anchors are not simply "towers first". They heal and reconstruct the target's shell, making it harder to wound and slower to act, while projecting zones at their bases that reduce interface damage and refuse terrain erosion. Breaking one exposes the interface and stops one share of healing, but also makes it act more often and extinguishes a patch of safe ground; its movement speed and the fixed collapse timer are unaffected by anchors either way. This choice should be argued about, not executed from the one correct guide.

The eight actions all revolve around *how the world explains the player*:

- The laser, the breath bolt and the sky lance turn space and ballistics into protocol echo. All three can be dodged by moving — at the cost of first reading who it is looking at.
- The grab-and-throw temporarily takes away the player's authority over their own position, but it must warn first and the whole carry must be visible: being lifted and being teleported are two completely different fears. The tendril flings the player clear of the body's silhouette before letting go; surviving the landing is the player's answer, not an ending the interface wrote for them.
- The tendril lash is the interface's only close-range language: it does not explain the player, it just pushes them away.
- Weapon impound and the gaze that empties the hotbar temporarily take over the item narrative. Impounded items leave a barrier placeholder that states its status and enter the recovery ledger; the hotbar sweep really drops the items on the ground where anyone can pick them up. The fear comes from losing control, not from things silently disappearing.
- Forced expulsion treats the connection itself as an attack, but it never selects the integrated-server host, and it can never let one client's sequence terminate a LAN world.

The World Interface may leave permanent scars on the End, but the scars must be bounded, explicable and clear of vanilla key structures and of the zones projected by surviving anchors. It cannot destroy a stability anchor, and it cannot use "meta" as licence to modify the player's world without limit.

## The two endings

### Success: cutting the interface before the explanation collapses

The player defeats the World Interface before the countdown ends. The altar opens a return exit, and the vanilla End-poem channel is used to present this run's success poem, credits and post-credits; the poem remembers whether they preserved all, some or none of the anchors. Only after the poem completes and the player really returns to the Overworld does the long-restricted view distance unlock from 6/12/16 (12 elsewhere) to 16.

This is not a return to before it happened: the battle scars remain, and the title-screen ending lock acknowledges that this world has ended. A successful save is described as *sealed*. From resolution onward, ordinary anomalies, gap pressure, decay and pursuits all fall permanently silent; no forged epilogue is added to the story.

### Failure: the interface crosses the client boundary

When the collapse finishes first, the World Interface enters escape resolution. The client is not closed: after the fixed sequence, the vanilla return path sends the player back to the Overworld, the entire local world renders as missing textures, and an on-screen line says the run is over. If the failing player is a published LAN host, the server still exists and their guests see unchanged blocks and rules.

Failure is not a destructive operation. The horror lives in client-side presentation and lossless markers; rewriting `level.dat`, regions or player data is not allowed. A failed save is described as *corrupted*. The player can leave from their own pause menu, and after a relaunch the Alpha presentation stays until F8 recovery completes.

## Ending locks, F8 and replaying

Both endings disable exactly three entry points on the title screen: Singleplayer, Multiplayer and Realms. Options, Accessibility and Quit remain — that is the boundary that lets the player leave and recover safely.

F8 opens its confirmation only when a persistent ending lock exists, and the wording matches the outcome. Confirming restores the desktop, Notepad, window and resource-pack state the mod owns, then closes normally. On the next launch the exactly-matching local save is isolated by a `.thefourthfrequency-corrupted` marker: success reads *sealed*, failure reads *corrupted*. A new story must start from a new world.

`-Dthefourthfrequency.safeMode=true` is a recovery back door for an interrupted sequence, not a narrative shortcut.

## Writing and audiovisual boundaries

- Terminal copy prefers short sentences: actionable information first, incomplete explanation second.
- Never substitute a fake system error for a rule hint; important controls must warn clearly.
- Horror comes from familiar systems being reinterpreted — not from silent save deletion, item theft or unbounded desktop changes.
- High contrast, flicker and peak volume are bounded by the first-run safety notice and by config.
- Meta effects may only change local state the mod explicitly owns or can restore. Server-authoritative multiplayer data is never contaminated by client hallucination.

Interface sequences (such as the weather tool's sky monitoring) are bound by three further hard rules:

- **Actionable information fails visibly; it never breaks silently.** Players walk home on "night in about N minutes", so an anomaly may take that number away — but it may not hand them another equally plausible one. Instrument readings carry no survival decision, which is exactly why they are free to spike and jitter.
- **Fault copy uses the instrument's own language; it never forges exceptions or stack traces.** A fake crash both violates the "no fake system errors" rule above and makes players think the game is genuinely broken. The horror is a device losing the sky, not a piece of software erroring out.
- **Exit paths may only be taken over by a one-off sequence with a definite end, and flicker stays under 3 Hz.** Page tabs, the back button and the close hint are never covered in any ordinary phase, and any visible state change is held for at least 7 ticks.

  The single exception is the boot sequence the first time a new save opens the terminal. It may keep the player in until it is done, but it must satisfy four testable conditions at once:

  1. **One-off.** A one-way server latch that never replays under any circumstance; existing saves are backfilled as "has already used the terminal" and do not get to watch it.
  2. **A definite end.** Four steps, each advanced by the player's own click. The client cannot advance it for them, nor settle the task because it "finished playing".
  3. **A safe release.** Any damage taken unlocks it immediately with progress preserved. The terminal does not pause the world, so "cannot be closed" during a fight means "cannot fight back".
  4. **Server-overridable.** A forced close always outranks the onboarding lock.

  While locked, the close hint must be replaced by an explanation that exit is currently unavailable. A screen that says "press Esc to exit" while Esc does nothing is exactly the "no silent failure" rule again. Any lock that is not one-off, has no end, or has no release condition is not permitted. These conditions are asserted by unit tests, not left to discipline.
